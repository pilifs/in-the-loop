# The protocol models

Two TLA+ models, each built around one of Quorum's state machines: [two-phase commit](#the-two-phase-commit-model) around the coordinator, and [Raft leader election](#the-raft-election-model) around the role machine and the vote rule. In both, the state machines are generated from the behavioural contracts of the Java, and everything else is written against the Java, action by action.

## The two-phase-commit model

A TLA+ model of two-phase commit around the Quorum coordinator: the participants, the coordinator, the votes in flight between them, and the two timeouts.
TLC explores every interleaving for the participants in `TwoPhaseCommit.cfg`, checks the invariants in every state it reaches, and then checks two temporal properties over every behaviour, the infinite ones included: that every participant eventually decides, and that the protocol implements Lamport and Gray's transaction-commit specification. A second configuration, `TwoPhaseCommitInductive.cfg`, checks that an invariant of the model is inductive, which proves atomicity for runs of any length. Two more let the coordinator crash: in `TwoPhaseCommitCrash.cfg` it may crash between steps, or after logging a phase change and before the broadcast goes out, and restarts on a recovery rule generated from `Recovery#decide`, the model's second generated operator, under the same invariants and properties; `TwoPhaseCommitCrashInductive.cfg` checks the inductive step with a crash and a restart among the steps. Two more let the participants crash: in `TwoPhaseCommitParticipantCrash.cfg` a participant may crash between steps, or after logging its vote and before sending it, and restarts on its own rule, generated from `Participant#decide`, the model's third generated operator, which also decides what a participant does when asked to prepare, when it gives up and when it reads the decision; a prepared participant holds its locks through a restart, which is what makes the protocol blocking. `TwoPhaseCommitParticipantCrashInductive.cfg` checks the inductive step with crashes of either process among the steps. A seventh, `TwoPhaseCommitTrace.cfg`, is trace validation: the Java nodes around the rules, the coordinator node with its log and its broadcast and the participant nodes with their logs and votes, are run through scripted scenarios with crashes of either process injected, the state after each step is recorded in the model's variables, and TLC walks each recorded run and holds every step to the model's next-state relation, which checks the glue the model otherwise takes on trust.
This is approach D1 in [`approaches.md`](../../../../approaches.md), applied to the second subject system; the [Box Office model](../../boxoffice/spec/README.md) describes the idea at length.

The invariants:

- **Atomicity.** Once the coordinator or any participant has committed, no participant has aborted: every participant that decides, decides the same way.
- **CommitFollowsTheCoordinator.** A participant commits only on the coordinator's decision, and only after voting yes.
- **TypeOK.**

The temporal properties, checked after the invariants:

- **EveryParticipantDecides.** Every participant eventually commits or aborts: `\A p \in Participants : <>(pstate[p] \in {"COMMITTED", "ABORTED"})`. A participant that voted yes holds its locks until it hears the decision, so this is the property that the coordinator always decides and that the decision reaches everyone. It is checked under the model's fairness conditions (`Fairness`): participants vote or give up, the coordinator counts every vote that reaches it and fires its timer while votes are missing, participants apply a decision once made, and a crashed coordinator restarts. Nothing is assumed about which vote a participant casts, when one gives up, or when the coordinator crashes, within the configuration's bound. A counterexample is an infinite behaviour, which TLC shows as a finite prefix and how it goes on: stuttering in the last state forever, or returning to an earlier state and repeating.
- **ImplementsTransactionCommit.** Every behaviour of the protocol, seen through the participants' states alone, is a behaviour of `TCommit.tla`: `TC!TCSpec` with `TC == INSTANCE TCommit`. TLC checks it as an action property, every step of the protocol against the next-state relation of transaction commit.

TLC does not say which temporal property a counterexample violates, so `jvmcontract tlc` checks them one at a time when the configuration lists several, and names the one that fails.

The inductive invariant, `Inductive`, is what makes atomicity true, written out: the invariants above and six more, about the votes counted and in flight and about what a crash can leave undone, which every reachable state agrees on and an arbitrary state need not.

- **VotesInFlightAreUncounted.** A vote on its way to the coordinator has not been counted, and a participant votes once.
- **DecisionMatchesThePhase.** What the coordinator broadcast, if anything, is what its phase says. A decided coordinator may have nothing out: a crash can fall between the phase change, which it logs, and the broadcast.
- **ParticipantsAgreeWithTheirVotes.** A working participant has not voted, a prepared one voted yes (unless a crash took the vote after the state was logged), a no voter has aborted, and a yes voter that aborted did so on the coordinator's say-so.
- **CommitNeedsEveryVote.** The coordinator commits only once every vote is counted and yes.
- **BroadcastOnceUp.** A coordinator that is up and decided has its decision out: it broadcast it when it decided, or on restarting. This is the conjunct a recovery rule must restore, and the one a restart with nothing to redo breaks.
- **DownOnlyAfterACrash.** The coordinator is down only after a crash, so that a configuration without crashes admits the same states as before.
- **ParticipantsDownOnlyAfterACrash.** Participants are down only after crashes: no more of them than have crashed.

`Atomicity` alone is not inductive: a state with the coordinator committed and a vote still in flight satisfies it, and one step later the vote is counted as a no. `Inductive` is, and `TwoPhaseCommitInductive.cfg` has TLC check it the way the [Raft model's](#the-raft-election-model) inductive configuration does: the invariant as the initial predicate, every state it admits, one step of `Next`, the conjuncts listed one by one so that TLC names the one that fails. It admits 736 states against 728 reachable, which is how close four conjuncts about the votes come to describing the protocol. The proof of `TwoPhase` in Lamport and Gray's paper rests on an invariant of the same shape, about the messages that have been sent; a message-passing model's invariant is inductive once it says what a message in flight means about its sender.

The crash configurations add the coordinator's log to the picture. The coordinator logs every change to its phase and its tally before it acts on it, so what a crash costs is the step in progress: `Perform`, which applies a decision, either logs the phase change and broadcasts, or (while `MaxCrashes` allows) logs the phase change and crashes, with the broadcast lost; `Crash` is a crash between steps, and `Recover` hands the logged phase to the recovery rule and applies its decision through the same `Perform`, so a restart can be cut short too. The recovery rule as committed is presumed abort (Mohan, Lindsay and Obermarck): a logged decision is sent again, since a participant that heard it ignores the repeat, and no logged decision aborts. `TwoPhaseCommitCrash.cfg` allows two crashes, enough for every order of a crash, a restart and a decision, and TLC checks the invariants, the liveness property and the refinement over 3,448 states; `TwoPhaseCommitCrashInductive.cfg` admits 2,728 states, the coordinator up or down, and checks that a crash and a restart land in states the invariant admits, which proves atomicity by induction for runs with crashes. The two configurations the model had set `MaxCrashes = 0` and keep their 728 and 736 states.

The participant-crash configurations add the participants' logs. The participant's rule, `Participant!Decide`, is the third generated operator, from the contract of `Participant#decide`, and every participant action goes through it: `Vote` asks it with `Prepare`, `ParticipantTimeout` with `GiveUp`, `Learn` with `Learned` and the decision, `ParticipantRecover` with `Restarted`, and `Act` carries the decision out, the state change logged and then the vote the effects say sent. A participant may crash after the log write, with the vote lost or not, or between steps (`ParticipantCrash`), while `MaxParticipantCrashes` allows; its restart is one step. The rule as committed has a prepared participant hold its locks through a restart, since it cannot decide alone (Skeen's blocking property), and one that had not voted abort, since it logs before it sends and so sent nothing. `TwoPhaseCommitParticipantCrash.cfg` allows one participant crash and one coordinator crash, enough for every order of a crash, a restart and a decision on either side, and TLC checks the invariants, the liveness property and the refinement over 12,754 states; `TwoPhaseCommitParticipantCrashInductive.cfg` admits 18,636 states, each process up or down, and checks the inductive step, for which `ParticipantsAgreeWithTheirVotes` allows a prepared participant whose vote a crash took, and `ParticipantsDownOnlyAfterACrash` ties the participants that are down to the count. The four configurations before them set `MaxParticipantCrashes = 0` and keep their states.

The trace configuration turns the model on the implementation. The model takes the glue around the rules on trust: that each process logs before it sends, that a crash loses nothing logged, that the tally the rule decides on is the tally, that a restart starts from the log. The Java nodes in [`twopc`](../src/main/java/net/terminalvalue/quorum/twopc) are that glue, written as a real system is: a `CoordinatorNode` that counts votes, asks the rules, writes its `CoordinatorLog` and broadcasts on the `Network`, `ParticipantNode`s that ask their rule, write their `ParticipantLog` and send their votes, and a `Faults` injector per process that kills it after any I/O operation. The test-side `Traces` class runs them through eleven scripted scenarios, a commit, an abort on a no, two on the timer, four with the coordinator crashing between steps, after a logged commit, after a logged abort and in the middle of a restart, and three with a participant crashing, after voting yes, before voting, and after logging its yes and before sending it, and records the state after each step in the model's nine variables, as the module `TwoPhaseCommitTraces.tla`. `TwoPhaseCommitTrace.tla` extends the model and walks each run: its own next-state relation loads the next recorded state, and the action property `EveryStepIsOneOfTheModel`, which is `[][Next]_vars`, asks of every step that it be an action of the model or a stutter. TLC reports a step the model has no action for with the run and the state it reached, as a counterexample like any other. The check is Cirstea, Kuppe, Loillier and Merz's trace validation in its simplest form, every variable recorded, and it covers the scripted runs and no others: the six configurations before it are what cover every interleaving. Writing it found one gap in the model: a restart with nothing to do, which a crash cuts short, is one recorded step and was two steps of the model, so `Perform` now lets a crash fall in any step, after what the step logged, which adds edges and no reachable states.

What the model leaves out: one transaction; messages can be reordered but not lost or duplicated; the coordinator's decision is a broadcast every participant can read; the coordinator's log holds its phase and its tally and a participant's its state, so a crash loses the step in progress and nothing else; a participant's restart is one step. A participant that voted yes cannot give up, and holds its locks through a restart, which is the blocking property of the protocol.

```bash

jvmcontract tla src/contract/Coordinator.decide.contract spec/CoordinatorContract.tla
jvmcontract tla src/contract/Recovery.decide.contract spec/RecoveryContract.tla
jvmcontract tla src/contract/Participant.decide.contract spec/ParticipantContract.tla
jvmcontract tlc spec/TwoPhaseCommit.tla --library ../../tools/tla/standard-modules
jvmcontract tlc spec/TwoPhaseCommit.tla --config spec/TwoPhaseCommitInductive.cfg --library ../../tools/tla/standard-modules
jvmcontract tlc spec/TwoPhaseCommit.tla --config spec/TwoPhaseCommitCrash.cfg --library ../../tools/tla/standard-modules
```

## The Raft election model

A TLA+ model of Raft leader election (Ongaro and Ousterhout, section 5.2) around the Quorum role machine and vote rule: the servers, their terms, votes and logs, and the RPCs between them.
TLC explores every interleaving for the servers and terms in `RaftElection.cfg`, checks the invariants in every state it reaches, and checks two action properties on every step. Six more configurations check the same model other ways: `RaftElectionInductive.cfg` checks that the invariant `Inductive` is inductive, `RaftElectionFour.cfg` checks four servers under a symmetry set, `RaftElectionLive.cfg` checks two liveness properties under fairness conditions, `RaftElectionLogs.cfg` gives one server the log a deposed leader left behind and checks that the election restriction keeps it from being elected, `RaftElectionDelayed.cfg` lets the reply to a vote request wait on the wire, so that the role machine is handed a reply from a term the candidate has left, and `RaftElectionHistory.cfg` has two history variables record every term a server has led and every vote cast, and checks what the paper claims of a whole run: at most one leader elected per term, and one vote per server per term.

The vote itself is the second generated operator, `Vote!Decide`, from the contract of `VoteRule#decide`: the rule proper, a total function from the voter's ballot and the candidate's request to a decision, which the Java's `VoteRule.grant` wraps with the arithmetic that reduces terms, ids and logs to how they compare with the voter's own. The model repeats that arithmetic in TLA+ (`OrderOf`, the ballot, `LogOrder`) and hands the result to the generated rule, as `grant` does; so a change to the rule reaches the model through the regenerated contract, and a change to the arithmetic does not, which is what the three [property proofs](../src/contract/properties.contract) of `grant` are for. The rule is applied to the voter as it then is, after the role machine has adopted a newer term, so that the rows for a candidate of a newer term are never exercised while the role machine does its job; example 059 is what happens when it stops doing so. The logs are fixed, `Logs`, since the model has no replication: every server holds an entry of term 1 and an entry of term 2 at index 2, except the servers in `LeftBehind`, which hold three entries of term 1, the log of a leader deposed before it committed them (figure 7 of the paper, server f). The configuration cannot write a function, so it names the servers and the module builds the logs. Examples 069 and 070 change the rule, one vote per term and the election restriction, and TLC finds two leaders of one term and a leader without the committed entry, the latter only under the configuration that leaves a server behind; example 071 changes the arithmetic, which the properties catch and the model cannot.

What the verdict rests on is measured rather than assumed: `jvmcontract tlc --coverage` asks TLC for its coverage statistics and reads them back against the generated operators, row by row (an arm of the `CASE` is evaluated when no earlier arm matched, so an arm was taken as often as its guard was evaluated minus the next arm's), and the example pages show, for each configuration, how many rows of each contract a step of the model takes and which none does. With three servers, steps take 27 of the 36 rows of the role machine and 4 of the 27 rows of the vote rule; with a server left behind, 12 of the vote rule's, since only then is there a log to compare. The rows no step takes are the model's abstractions written out: the vote rule's nine rows for a newer term (the role machine adopts it first), its six for a ballot cast for the candidate asking (an RPC is one step, so no candidate asks a voter twice), the role machine's rows for a follower counting a majority (a tally is counted in the step that fills it, and emptied before if the server steps down) and, under every configuration but the delayed one, for a follower or a stale candidate taking a reply (replies arrive in the step of the request). A change to the Java that lands only on such rows is verified whatever it does, and the generator labels the verdict vacuous: examples 072 and 074 are two such changes, a repeat request refused and a follower that leads on a late majority, and the resolution each page gives is a test, a property or a bigger model, not the verdict. Example 073 makes the nine newer-term rows throw, which are then meant never to be taken, and `KernelNeverThrows` is what proves that no run can take them. The inductive step counts separately: it starts from every state the invariant admits, not from a run, and takes two more rows of the vote rule (a ballot cast for a candidate whose tally lacks it, the lost reply the invariant allows for). The delayed configuration is the bigger model for the reply rows: with a vote reply on the wire as a step of its own, a candidate that has timed out, stepped down or won takes a reply from the term it was in, and steps take 33 of the role machine's 36 rows, the three left being a follower or a leader counting a majority and the leader's own exception. Examples 081 to 083 are three changes to the rows for a reply from an older term, which every other configuration verifies vacuously: a role machine that adopts such a term, which sends a term backwards; one that treats such a reply as impossible and throws, which is the one-step RPC's assumption written into the Java; and one that logs it, verified.

The invariants:

- **ElectionSafety.** At most one leader per term (figure 3 of the paper).
- **LeaderHasAMajority.** A leader holds the votes of a majority of the servers, its own among them.
- **LeaderCompleteness.** A leader's log holds every committed entry (figure 3 of the paper), an entry being committed once a majority of the servers hold it. The election restriction is what makes it so: a candidate needs votes from a majority, a voter grants only a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own, and some voter holds the entry. Checked for the logs of the configuration, which nothing changes: `RaftElectionLogs.cfg` is a witness that a vote rule without the restriction elects a leader that lacks the entry, not a proof over every log a cluster could build.
- **KernelNeverThrows.** No timeout or RPC any reachable state allows makes the role machine or the vote rule throw. The Java throws `IllegalStateException` on one input, a leader hearing a leader of its own term, which is a claim that the input never occurs. Each action computes its next state as a record that says whether a decision it goes through is `Throws`, and takes no such step; the invariant says there is never one to take, so the claim is checked rather than assumed, in every configuration. The inductive configuration lists it as well, so that TLC says whether `Inductive` implies it: on the committed kernel it does, by election safety.
- **AtMostOneLeaderPerTerm** and **AtMostOneVotePerTerm**, checked by `RaftElectionHistory.cfg`. Election safety as figure 3 of the paper states it, at most one leader can be elected in a given term, and the rule of section 5.2 that it rests on, a server votes for at most one candidate per term, are claims about a run, and `ElectionSafety` is what a state can show: it holds, as does `LeaderHasAMajority`, in a run in which the leader of a term steps down and the term then elects another, two leaders of one term, never at once. The state forgets: a server's vote is gone the moment it moves to a newer term, and a leader that has stepped down is a follower like any other. With `History = TRUE`, two history variables remember, `elected` every pair of a server and a term it has led, and `voted` every triple of a voter, a term and a candidate; each step adds the leaders and the votes of the state it lands in, and nothing else about the run changes (Lamport and Merz, [Auxiliary Variables in TLA+](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/auxiliary.pdf)). The invariants say that no two pairs share a term and no voter has two triples of one term with different candidates. They are checked over runs, not by induction: the state does not determine the history, so `Inductive` says nothing about it, and the inductive configuration leaves the recording off.
- **TypeOK.**

The action properties, `[][A]_vars` formulas that TLC checks on every step rather than on every state:

- **TermsNeverDecrease.** `[][\A s \in Servers : currentTerm'[s] >= currentTerm[s]]_vars`.
- **LeadersNeverCampaign.** `[][\A s \in Servers : role[s] = "LEADER" => role'[s] # "CANDIDATE"]_vars`: a leader leaves office as the follower of a newer term, or by stepping down, never by starting an election against itself. The states on either side of such a step are states the protocol allows, a leader in one term and a candidate in the next, which is why no invariant can state this and the model states it as a property of steps. TLC reports an action property by name, and its counterexample is the run up to the offending step.

The inductive invariant, `Inductive`, is what makes election safety true, written out: the invariants above and five more, each a reason two leaders cannot share a term.

- **VotesAreBound.** A vote in a tally was cast in the tally's term: the voter is still in that term and voted for the tally's owner, or has since moved to a newer term.
- **TalliesOfATermAreDisjoint.** No server's vote is in two tallies of one term.
- **CampaignersVoteForThemselves.** A candidate or leader voted for itself in its term, and holds its own vote.
- **TalliesHoldTheirOwnersVote.** A tally is empty, or holds its owner's vote and its owner voted for itself.
- **TermZeroIsQuiet.** Nothing happens in term 0.

`ElectionSafety` alone is not inductive: a state with two candidates of one term, each one vote short, satisfies it, and one step does not. `Inductive` is: from any state it admits, every step of `Next` lands in a state it admits, which `RaftElectionInductive.cfg` has TLC check by using the invariant as the initial predicate (Lamport, [Using TLC to Check Inductive Invariance](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/tla/inductive-invariant.pdf)). Since `Init` satisfies it and it implies `ElectionSafety`, that proves election safety by induction, for every run of the configured cluster however long; it is the form of argument that carries over to any cluster size, where exploring the runs does not. `InductiveInit` is the same predicate written so that TLC can enumerate it: each variable drawn from its type, each conjunct placed right after the last variable it mentions, so that a partial state is dropped as soon as one fails. A counterexample is one step from a state that need not be reachable, so it is either a bug or a conjunct too strong for a correct change, and the configurations that run from `Init` tell which. The conjuncts are listed one by one in the configuration, so that TLC names the one that fails.

The liveness properties, checked by `RaftElectionLive.cfg` under `Fairness`, which says what each server is assumed to get round to: a server whose election timer nothing resets times out (a leader's timer is off, and a leader's heartbeats, when the server accepts them, reset it: `Unled`), a candidate with a vote to ask for asks, a leader with a heartbeat to send sends it. Nothing is assumed about which timer fires first or how often.

- **ALeaderIsElected.** `WithinTheBudget => <>(\E s \in Servers : role[s] = "LEADER")`: a run in which every term stays below `MaxTerm` elects a leader. With no leader, every server's timer keeps firing and each firing starts a term, so the only runs without a leader are the ones that spend the budget; a run in which the terms stop and no leader comes is a cluster that has stopped trying. The condition is what a model without a clock can state: Raft elects a leader by randomizing the timeouts so that eventually one fires alone, and every term can split, so no number of terms is enough in every run. The property is not vacuous: the stronger `\A s : WithinTheBudget => <>(role[s] = "LEADER")` fails, so runs within the budget exist.
- **StaleLeadersStepDown.** `(role[l] = "LEADER" /\ \E s : currentTerm[s] > currentTerm[l]) ~> role[l] # "LEADER"`: a leader whose term the cluster has left steps down, told by its heartbeat to a server with a newer term, or by that server's request for a vote.

Each fairness condition is weak fairness, `WF_vars(A)`, written out as TLA+ defines it: `<>[]CanA => []<><<A>>_vars`, if `A` is enabled from some point on, it happens infinitely often, with the enabling condition stated as a predicate (`CanTimeout`, `CanCanvass`, `CanHeartbeat`: the action's guard, and a step that changes some variable). Written with `WF_vars` itself, the check took TLC a minute on 610 states: `ENABLED` of an action with the role machine inside it is computed symbolically, once per step of the liveness check. The predicates are the same condition evaluated directly, each action computes its next state as a record (`TimeoutNext` and the others) so that the step and the predicate are one computation, and `RaftElectionEnabled.cfg` checks that the predicates agree with `ENABLED` in every reachable state. The liveness configuration has no symmetry set, since TLC's liveness checking is unsound under one.

What the model leaves out: replication, so the logs are the configuration's and a heartbeat carries no entries, and the election restriction is checked for those logs alone; message delay and loss, since an RPC is one step, so a server always answers with the term it has now, a stale server learns of a newer term the moment it talks to one that has it, no reply is lost and no candidate asks twice, except under `RaftElectionDelayed.cfg`, where a vote reply waits on the wire as long as the model likes, a reply never taken is one the network lost, and one request per pair of servers is outstanding at a time; and time, which is why the liveness property is conditioned on the term budget. Terms stop at `MaxTerm`, past which no timer fires. The coverage of the contract rows, above, is the list of what these abstractions cost.
